RIGHTS OF WAY AND ACCESS: A PRACTITIONER'S GUIDE
Firm library reference, second edition

This guide is fictional training material written for the Lexo demonstration. It does not describe the law of any real jurisdiction and must not be relied on as legal advice. The rules, time periods and figures it contains were invented so that answers drawn from it can be checked against the text.

CHAPTER 1: WHAT A PRIVATE RIGHT OF WAY IS

1.1 A private right of way allows the owner of one piece of land, the benefited land, to pass over a neighbour's land, the burdened land, for a particular purpose. The right belongs to the land rather than to any individual, so it passes automatically to each new owner when the benefited land is sold.

1.2 The burdened owner keeps ownership of the route and may continue to use it, provided that use does not substantially interfere with the right.

1.3 A right of way must be distinguished from a personal permission, sometimes called a licence. A permission can be withdrawn at any time, does not pass to a buyer, and does not count towards acquiring a right by long use.

CHAPTER 2: HOW RIGHTS OF WAY ARISE

2.1 Express grant. A right of way may be created in a deed or transfer that describes the route and what it may be used for. Where the deed contains a plan, the route shown on the plan prevails over a written description if the two conflict.

2.2 Implication. Where land is divided and part of it is sold, a right of way may be implied over the retained part if it is necessary for the reasonable enjoyment of the part sold. A right will not be implied merely because it would be convenient.

2.3 Long use. A right of way may be acquired by use that has been open, without force and without permission, for an unbroken period. For the purposes of this guide, the required period is twenty-two years, measured back from the date proceedings are issued.

2.4 Use that relies on the burdened owner's permission does not count towards the twenty-two years. Nor does use that was secret, or use that continued only by breaking through a locked gate or fence.

CHAPTER 3: PROVING LONG USE

3.1 The firm's evidence checklist for long use is as follows: dated photographs of the route in use; statements from at least two independent witnesses who can speak to the use across the whole period; any correspondence in which the burdened owner objected to, or permitted, the use; and historic maps or plans showing the route.

3.2 A gap in use of more than eighteen months should be treated as a serious weakness and flagged to the supervising partner straight away.

3.3 Use by visitors, tradespeople and delivery drivers counts as use by the benefited owner, provided they were coming to the benefited land.

CHAPTER 4: THE SCOPE OF THE RIGHT

4.1 The extent of a right of way is limited by how it arose. A right granted for access on foot does not permit vehicles. A right granted for "all purposes" permits vehicles of any kind reasonably suited to the route.

4.2 A right acquired by long use is limited to the kind and intensity of use proved over the qualifying period. A significant increase in use may exceed the right. Converting a single house into six flats which all rely on the same track is the standard example. The burdened owner may object to the excess but cannot block the original use.

4.3 Width. Where the grant does not state a width, the route is as wide as is reasonably necessary for the permitted use. For vehicular access, the firm treats a width of less than 2.7 metres at any point as a likely obstruction.

4.4 Parking. A right of way is a right to pass, not to stop. Parking on the route, even briefly, is not permitted by a right of way unless the grant expressly includes it.

CHAPTER 5: OBSTRUCTION

5.1 An obstruction is actionable only if it substantially interferes with the reasonable exercise of the right. The test is whether the right can still be practically and conveniently used, not whether it is as convenient as before.

5.2 Gates. A locked gate is normally an obstruction unless every person entitled to use the route has been given a key. A gate that is merely closed, but unlocked, is usually not an obstruction. A gate fitted with a keypad is treated like a locked gate unless the code has been given to every person entitled to use the route.

5.3 Planters, bollards and parked vehicles that narrow the route below the width needed for the permitted use are obstructions.

5.4 Letter before action. Before issuing proceedings, the firm's practice is to send a letter before action giving the other side twenty-eight days to remove the obstruction and to propose mediation.

5.5 Remedies. The court may grant a declaration of the right, an injunction requiring removal of the obstruction, and damages for the period during which the right was obstructed. Where an obstruction has stood for more than three years without complaint, the court is more likely to award damages instead of an injunction.

5.6 Self-help. The benefited owner may remove an obstruction themselves only if it can be done without a breach of the peace and only to the extent necessary to pass. The firm does not recommend self-help, and fee earners should advise clients against it in writing.

CHAPTER 6: REPAIR AND MAINTENANCE

6.1 Unless the grant says otherwise, the burdened owner has no duty to repair the route.

6.2 The benefited owner is entitled, but not obliged, to carry out reasonable repairs at their own cost, and may enter the burdened land to do so after giving at least seven days' written notice.

6.3 Where several owners share a route, the firm recommends a written maintenance agreement dividing costs in proportion to use.

CHAPTER 7: ENDING A RIGHT OF WAY

7.1 A right of way may end by express release in a deed, by the benefited and burdened land coming into the same ownership, or by abandonment.

7.2 Abandonment requires clear evidence of an intention never to use the right again. Non-use alone, even for many years, is not enough. Building a wall across the only point of access, with the benefited owner's agreement, is strong evidence of an intention to abandon.

CHAPTER 8: PRACTICAL TIPS

8.1 Always obtain the title documents for both properties at the outset. An express grant will usually settle the dispute without any need to prove long use.

8.2 Walk the route with the client if possible, and measure its width at its narrowest point.

8.3 Record any signs, gates or barriers, and note when each appeared. The date a gate was first locked is often the most important fact in an obstruction case.

8.4 Encourage early mediation. In the firm's experience, most right of way disputes settle once both sides see a clear plan of the route and its permitted use.
